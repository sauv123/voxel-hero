Case study · olo

A social-confidence app with no audience.

Social and habit apps grow through visibility: feeds, streaks, leaderboards. For people with social anxiety, that visibility is exactly why they quit. I led a two-person team for two months to answer one question: what does an app that helps you talk to people look like once you take the audience out?

The problem

The people who most need social practice avoid the apps built for it, because those apps put them on show.

What I owned

Product direction, the scope calls below, the usability test plan, and the challenge-screen system.

The hardest call

Cutting the community feed, and with it the main way olo could have connected people.

Result (honest)

Testing with 4 people changed 4 core features. There's no live build, so no usage data. One big question is still open.

Situation

Everything that makes social apps grow is what makes anxious users leave.

We audited three apps that ask strangers to interact: BeReal, Randonautica and Meetup. The same mechanics that drive engagement for confident users (public posting windows, open group chats, visible activity) were the top complaints from anxious ones. Our empathy research said the same thing in the users' own words: being watched is the trigger, not a lack of motivation.

What was unclear going in

  1. Can you build social confidence with almost no social features?→ Decisions 01 and 03
  2. How much push helps before it becomes pressure?→ Decision 02
  3. Can one challenge fit people who range from "extremely shy" to "mostly fine"?→ Smaller calls
What I aimed to change

Get an anxious first-time user to do one real-world social action, without feeling watched.

Not daily active users, and not time in app. The product succeeds when someone puts their phone down and says hi to a barista.

2 months 2 designers, no engineers No live build 4 test participants 18 survey responses

Decisions

Three calls that shaped the product, and what each one cost.

Decision 01 · Product direction

A map of nearby strangers, or a private companion?

Situation
Research gave us two concepts pulling in opposite directions. One connected people fast but exposed them. The other felt safe but risked becoming a journal that never got anyone out of the house.
Options
  • A
    Live map of nearby, comfort-matched people with buddy mode. The fastest route to real connection, but you're visible to strangers.
  • B
    Private companion with a diary, event radar and calming sounds. Safe, but passive.
  • C
    Hybrid: B's private loop, A's mood-matching, and real-world micro-challenges as the bridge between the two.Chosen
Trade-off
I cut the map and the event radar, so olo can't introduce you to anyone. It gets you ready to talk to people who are already around you.
Evidence
Visibility was the most common threat in the SWOT across all three competitors, and the main trigger in the empathy map.
Decision 02 · Pressure

Keep a short countdown on challenges, or remove it?

Situation
The challenge screen we tested had a 2-minute countdown. The idea was that a deadline gives structure and gets people to finish, which is a standard habit-app lever.
Options
  • A
    Keep the timer and soften the copy.
  • B
    Make it opt-in for users who want a push.
  • C
    Remove timers everywhere. Show an estimated duration chip as information only, not a deadline.Chosen
Trade-off
We lost our only in-the-moment nudge. If someone hesitates mid-challenge, nothing pushes them over the line.
Evidence
3 of 4 testers felt uncomfortable or pressured by the countdown. Even a short one read as a deadline.Usability testing, 4 participants
Decision 03 · Social layer

Soften the community feed, or cut it?

Situation
The prototype we tested had a feed: seeing others' progress and reacting to posts. It was the one place where olo users met each other, and every competitor had one.
Options
  • A
    Keep the feed, with anonymous posting.
  • B
    Friends-only feed with no reactions or counts.
  • C
    Cut it. Give its home-screen space to Safe Zone filters and Cultural Tips.Chosen
Trade-off
No social proof and no community. Testers still said they wanted connection, so this call left a real need unmet. See the learning under Result.
Evidence
"Not of importance right now." All 4 testers independently valued Safe Zone and Cultural Tips, features we'd treated as secondary.Usability testing, 4 participants

What I'd do differently

I solved "visible", and gave up on "social" along with it.

Cutting the feed was the right call for safety. But testers still asked for connection, and I treated "no visible social features" as if it meant "no social features". The next step is an opt-in, low-visibility way to meet one other person. That isn't a feed; it's closer to Concept A's buddy mode, with stricter controls.

If it shipped: targets, not results

What I'd measure

  • First-challenge completionThe share of new users who finish "Befriending the Mirror" in their first session.
  • Step-up rateHow many users move from easy to medium challenges within two weeks.
  • Return after a skipped dayWithout streaks, does a missed day still end the habit?

Limitations: 4 usability participants and 18 survey responses give a direction, not statistical proof. No formal WCAG audit was run yet.

The product

Five steps, and no feed.

How it works: tap a step to see how olo moves someone from a mood check to a real-world moment and back to private reflection.

01. Mood check-in

olo opens with six plain-language states (Calm, Nervous, Energetic, Sluggish, Drained, Alert) instead of a 1–10 slider, which testers read as clinical. The state you pick shapes everything that follows.

Onboarding also asks what brings you here (build confidence, prepare for conversations, make 1–2 friends) and what feels hardest, so day-one challenges are personalised, not generic.

02. A matched challenge

Home surfaces quests based on your patterns, for example that you do better in the evenings or that cafés feel safer than open spaces, and groups them by real-world place, like “Local Coffee Shop: 2 quests”.

Every challenge has a difficulty tier (easy / med / deep, shown as a sprout growing into a tree) and a duration chip, so the ask always fits what you picked at check-in.

03. The real-world action

Every new user's first quest is “Befriending the Mirror”: practising a greeting alone before trying it on a stranger. From there, challenges scale from saying hi to small talk to complimenting a coworker.

Tips are concrete, not motivational: “start with people you see regularly”, “you don't need a full conversation; a nod counts”.

04. Private reflection

After a challenge, users record a short voice note or tag how it went. It stays private and is never posted anywhere.

From testing: “It was nerve-wracking at first, but the barista was really friendly. I felt proud afterwards.”

05. Visualised growth

Progress shows as a puzzle filling in, one piece per completed challenge, instead of a leaderboard or a streak counter.

Your Space turns reflections into self-knowledge: best times of day, safest places, and a notification tone you choose yourself (Gentle only / Moderate / Brave).

05Colour researchEach accent colour tied to a published finding+

Green sits at the centre of the visible spectrum and needs the least adjustment to perceive, so it reads as restful.

Blue is the most consistently calming colour across studies.

06Challenge-screen iterationsFrom one screen (V1) to a system (V4)+

V1 was content-first and text-heavy. V2 introduced the structure that stayed: chips, numbered steps, tips. V3 added illustration and affirmation copy. V4 applied the same structure to a second challenge type to prove it scales.

07Usability test protocol7 tasks, 3 criteria, 4 participants matched to the personas+
  1. Complete onboarding
    Do users understand how we gauge comfort zones and social energy?
  2. Log your mood
    Is emotional capture simple?
  3. Explore the home screen
    Where do users naturally click?
  4. Attempt a daily challenge
    Is it clear and emotionally comfortable?
  5. React to a post in the feed
    Does it trigger fear of judgement?
  6. Use the QR connect flow
    Social comfort and usability
  7. Check progress and settings
    Is the information hierarchy right?
