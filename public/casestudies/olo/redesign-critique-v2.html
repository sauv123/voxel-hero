Would I Interview This Candidate?

Yes, comfortably — for mid-level. Borderline for a senior IC role, and here's the specific gap that decides it. The research is now real and defensible: an empathy map with verbatim quotes, a competitive SWOT with named apps, testing findings that changed an actual decision (the timer). That's no longer decoration — it's evidence. But two things still read as junior-to-mid rather than senior:

  • No individual contribution line. This is a two-person team (Cosima Wagner + Sauveer Sinha) and every section reads as "we." A senior hiring manager's first question in the interview will be "what did you specifically own?" — and right now the case study hasn't pre-answered it.
  • Honesty about limitations is good, but under-leveraged. Naming the small sample size and missing accessibility audit is the right instinct — most candidates hide this. But a senior-level version would show the mitigation plan already scoped (who, how, by when) rather than just a bullet list at the bottom.
  • Strength: The Naming & Concept Exploration section is genuinely rare — almost no portfolio shows survey data on rejected app names. Keep this; it's memorable and differentiated in a 5-minute skim.
  • Strength: The "3 of 4 testers hated the timer → timer removed" story is the single best beat in the whole case study. It's concrete, falsifiable, and shows research changing a real decision. Everything else should be edited toward that same standard of specificity.

Top 10 Weaknesses (Round 2)

  1. No "my role" breakdown in a two-person project HIGH
    Every section says "we." A hiring manager evaluating an individual can't tell what Sauveer specifically drove vs. Cosima. This is the single highest-leverage fix available.
  2. No executive summary above the fold HIGH
    The hero states the concept but not the outcome. A recruiter skimming for 30 seconds should see the headline result (e.g. "testing directly removed a core mechanic and reshaped the home screen") before they ever scroll to Reflection.
  3. Limitations are disclosed but not mitigated MEDIUM
    "No accessibility audit yet" and "small sample" are listed as known gaps — good instinct — but nothing shows a scoped plan to close them. Senior work names the gap AND the fix.
  4. No live or clickable prototype MEDIUM
    Every screen is a static image behind a lightbox. Reviewers increasingly expect at least one tappable Figma/InVision link to judge interaction quality first-hand, not just the visual layer.
  5. Naming section is compelling but overlong LOW
    Four survey charts plus two landing-page mockups is a lot of real estate for a supporting beat. Trim to the single strongest chart and the final name reveal; move the rest to an appendix or "see more" toggle.
  6. Testing metrics are presented as goals, which reads ambiguously on a skim MEDIUM
    The "Targets we tested against" framing is honest, but a fast reader can mistake goal thresholds for achieved results. Needs a sharper visual distinction (e.g. "TARGET" watermark or a clearly separate "Actual" column, even if "actual" says "not yet measured").
  7. No accessibility work shown, only promised MEDIUM
    Even a partial pass — a manual contrast check on the sage/cream palette, one screen reader walkthrough — would move this from "we know we should" to "here's what we found." Right now it's a stated gap with zero evidence of effort.
  8. Videos have no caption/context card explaining what to look for LOW
    The walkthrough and brand-film videos are dropped in with only a small tag ("Product walkthrough"/"Brand film"). A reviewer who won't press play needs a one-line synopsis of what the video proves.
  9. Two-person team credit isn't visually distinguished anywhere else in the page LOW
    Cosima's name appears once, in the hero metadata, then never again — including in the concept-exploration section where her concept (Concept 2) is directly discussed. Attribution should travel with the work, not just sit in the header.
  10. Long single-page scroll with no progress/reading-time indicator LOW
    13 sections is a lot for a 5-minute reviewer. A slim progress bar or "8 min read" indicator helps someone decide how much attention to allocate before they start.

Section-by-Section Analysis

Hero + Context CardB-

Condensed metadata row (Role / Team / Timeline / Type) is exactly the "context card" pattern recommended in round one — correctly implemented.

Still no outcome statement. The deck sentence describes the product, not what the project proved.

Fix: Add one line under the deck: "Testing directly reversed three shipped decisions — including cutting a feature that scored highest on paper." Specific, falsifiable, and earns the scroll.
ProblemA-

Verbatim empathy-map quotes ("rehearsing a sentence three times") replace last round's generic problem statement. This is real evidence, properly cited by artifact type.

The SWOT callout paragraph is still a summary of a summary — it describes the pattern without showing a single concrete example from the SWOT board itself.

Fix: Pull one specific labeled sticky note from the SWOT (e.g. a named weakness under BeReal) into a small inline quote, the same way the empathy map quotes are handled.
ResearchA-

Method chips (empathy mapping, affinity mapping, POV, HMW) plus four named personas with distinct archetypes is a real methodology trail, not a slide of screenshots.

Persona descriptions are one-liners with no behavioral driver or quote — thinner than the empathy map that precedes them, which is a step down in evidence quality right when the reader expects it to go up.

Fix: Give each persona one concrete behavioral detail pulled from the actual research board (even paraphrased), matching the specificity of the Problem section.
Naming & Concept ExplorationB+

This is the standout addition versus round one. Real survey percentages, two rejected landing-page concepts, an honest account of why "Bloom" and "Elo" failed. Nothing else in the portfolio proves iteration this convincingly.

It's the longest section in the case study for a supporting beat — four bar-chart blocks plus two full mockup cards risks losing the 5-minute reviewer right when momentum matters most.

Fix: Keep the "Bloom vs. Elo understanding" chart and the final name reveal; compress the two relevance-percentage charts into a single sentence with the key numbers inline. Move the two landing-page mockups into a single side-by-side card.
Decisions & Strategic CutsA

The "3 of 4 testers felt pressured by the timer → timer removed" line is the best single piece of evidence in the entire case study — concrete, sourced, and it demonstrates research overruling a shipped decision. This is exactly what round one's critique asked for and couldn't get without fabrication.

It's currently buried as one row in a six-row table. A finding this strong deserves its own visual moment, not a table cell.

Fix: Pull it out as a standalone callout card with before/after screen thumbnails, and cross-link it from the hero's new outcome line.
Design SystemB+

Citing actual studies (Akers et al. 2012, Stone 2003, Elliot & Aarts 2011, Yildirim et al. 2011) for color choices, plus a real Duolingo/Headspace/Calm feature benchmark, is a genuinely senior move — design rationale backed by literature, not vibes.

The stated design-system maturity outpaces the actual accessibility evidence — contrast, color-blind safety, and screen-reader support are asserted by omission, not verified.

Fix: Add one real contrast-ratio table for the five core colors against the cream background — even a manual WebAIM check — so "we care about accessibility" has one piece of proof.
Prototype EvolutionA-

Correctly re-captioned from round one (the original mismatched the actual screenshots). V4 showing the same anatomy scaled to a second challenge type is a real systems-thinking signal — most candidates only show "it got prettier," not "it got reusable."

No real issue here — this section now does what it should. Lowest-priority section to touch further.

Solution WalkthroughB+

Real microcopy ("Befriending the Mirror," the actual reflection quote from testing) makes this feel like a shipped product, not a mockup.

Cosima's Concept 2 contributions (diary, calming sounds, event radar) are visible in the final product's DNA but never explicitly reconnected here — the reader loses the thread between "who proposed this" and "what shipped."

Fix: One small inline note per step where relevant: "(from Concept 2)" — cheap to add, closes the individual-contribution gap partially.
Usability TestingB

Real 7-task protocol, real evaluation criteria, and findings attributed to actual test patterns (not invented percentages) — this is the section round one most needed and fabricated instead.

The "targets we tested against" metrics sit visually identical to the findings below them. A skimming reviewer could easily read a goal ("<10% report discomfort") as an achieved result.

Fix: Label the metrics grid explicitly as "Rubric (not yet measured against a live product)" and consider muting its color treatment relative to the real findings section.
Reflection & LimitationsB+

This section is the biggest philosophical win over round one. Naming the sample size, admitting there's no live-product data, and calling the physical cards "unvalidated" instead of hiding it — this is what makes the whole case study credible rather than suspicious.

Each limitation is stated but not resourced. "No accessibility audit was run" reads differently depending on whether it's followed by nothing (as now) or "— scoped for two days once a build exists."

Fix: Add a one-line mitigation to each of the four limitations. Costs almost nothing to write, meaningfully changes how senior the section reads.
Next StepsA-

Explicitly labeling the physical puzzle cards as "unvalidated idea, not a roadmap commitment" is exactly the kind of self-aware scoping a senior reviewer wants to see — most portfolios present every future idea with false confidence.

Only refinement: visually separate the validated-research-backed items (accessibility pass, wider sample, quiet connection pilot) from the speculative one, rather than one flat numbered list.

Visual Design Recommendations

Typography & Hierarchy

Current: Baloo Bhai 2 (display) + Nunito Sans (body), warm cream palette — thematically correct for a "calm introvert-first" product, unlike round one's colder Playfair/Inter swap which fought the product's own design system.

Add one more size step between section-title and body copy for the At-a-Glance strip and the new "My Role" callout — both need to read as more urgent/scannable than a standard paragraph.

Layout & Composition

Current: Consistent 1200px container, generous section padding, card-based grids throughout. Reads as intentional, not templated.

Introduce a slim sticky "read progress" bar (a 3px accent-colored line under the nav that fills as the user scrolls) — cheap, modern, and helps a time-boxed reviewer self-pace through 13 sections.

Color & Motion

Current: Sage/terracotta/periwinkle used with clear semantics (sage = primary, terracotta = warning/cut, periwinkle = informational) — the exact discipline round one's critique asked for and got.

The reveal-on-scroll animation is tasteful and not overused. One addition worth considering: a subtle color shift on the "Decisions" section background (slightly warmer) to visually flag it as the case study's strongest evidence section — visual emphasis matching narrative emphasis.

Final Hiring Verdict

Round 1 score: 4/10. Round 2 score: 7/10 (Storytelling: 7, UX Process: 8, Product Thinking: 7, Visual Design: 7, Research: 8, Presentation: 7, Seniority: 6, Hire Potential: 7).

Would I interview this candidate? Yes. This now reads as strong mid-level work with clear senior instincts (honest limitations, real citations, a decision that testing actually reversed). It is not yet unambiguously senior — that gap is entirely about attribution and self-scoped mitigation, not about the quality of the underlying work.

What would tip this from "interview" to "fast-track"? Three cheap edits: a My Role callout, an outcome sentence in the hero, and a mitigation line under each stated limitation. None of these require new research — they require presenting the research that already exists with more confidence about what it proves.
