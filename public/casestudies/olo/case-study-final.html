Case Study

Designing for the Quiet.

olo is a calm, introvert-first mobile experience that helps people build real-world social confidence through mood-adaptive micro-challenges and private reflection — designed instead of the loud, streak-driven playbook most social apps copy from each other.

The Problem

Social apps are built for people who don't struggle to be social.

Most social and habit-building apps run on the same playbook: public visibility, streaks, and constant nudges to perform. That playbook rewards extroversion. For someone who over-thinks a simple "hi," it does the opposite of help — it adds an audience to an already anxious moment.

Empathy Map — Says

"Is now a good time? No, they look busy. I'll wait." — rehearsing a sentence three times before saying it out loud.

Empathy Map — Does

Pulls out phone immediately when feeling awkward, using it as a shield. Unlocks and re-locks it repeatedly without reading anything.

Empathy Map — Feels

Heart racing, palms sweating, shallow breathing. "Vulnerable... hopeful but terrified of rejection."

Empathy Map — Pain

"Not knowing what to say after 'Hello.' The awkward silence." Worrying the other person will read effort as being "creepy."

Where existing apps make it worse

A SWOT audit of three apps that ask strangers to interact — BeReal, Randonautica, and Meetup — surfaced a consistent pattern: the same mechanics that drive engagement for confident users (public posting windows, open group chats, visible activity feeds) show up as the top complaints from anxious ones. Visibility and time pressure were the recurring threats across all three.

Concept & Naming

Two concepts, three names, and a lot of second-guessing.

Most case studies skip straight to the finished name. Ours didn't start clean — we sketched two competing product concepts and survey-tested three candidate names before "olo" earned its place.

Concept 1

A live map of nearby, comfort-matched people

Emoji/color signals show social comfort level on a map; badge verification and "buddy mode" reduce risk; small invite-only groups replace open feeds.

Concept 2

A gamified companion with a diary and event radar

Mood check-in, general tips, a private diary, an "event radar" for upcoming meetups, and calming sounds/breathing exercises before an interaction.

What we kept from each

The mood-based matching and calm, low-visibility framing from Concept 1 became the emotional core; the diary/reflection loop and grounding exercises from Concept 2 became the "Reflect" step. The live map and public event radar — both of which reintroduce visibility — were cut.

Naming round 1

"ELO" — Find your people, at your pace.

Read as competent but slightly cold — and, for an anti-competition app, "Elo" carries an uncomfortable echo of ranking/rating systems.

Naming round 2

"BloomMeet" — Grow Confidence. Start Conversations.

Warmer, but "Bloom" leaned therapeutic/self-help — it undersold that this is about actually going outside and talking to someone.

olo

small hellos, real connections. Short, soft, and impossible to read as a competition or a diagnosis — which is exactly why it survived where "Bloom," "Elo," and "Bloomr" didn't.

Design System

A visual language for calm.

We benchmarked Duolingo, Headspace, and Calm feature-by-feature, then deliberately took the calm-and-minimal half of that spectrum — Headspace's reassuring tone and Calm's restraint — while rejecting Duolingo's aggressive, high-pressure gamification.

Color, backed by the literature

Every accent color was chosen against a specific finding, not a mood board.

Green sits at the center of the visible spectrum and requires the least adjustment to perceive — restful by nature.

Akers et al. (2012)

Blue is the most consistently calming color across studies — lowers blood pressure, heart rate and respiration.

Stone (2003)

Warm, muted orange-terracotta reads as sociable and enthusiastic without red's stimulating, alarm-like intensity.

Elliot & Aarts (2011)

Yellow boosts serotonin and mood, but overused reads as arousing or anxiety-inducing — used sparingly for optimism, not for alerts.

Yildirim et al. (2011)
External reading — "Do we feel colours? A systematic review" · PubMed Central
External reading — "A Guide to Designing for Introverts" · Smashing Magazine, Pratik Joglekar

Squircles & a plant-growth metaphor

Every interactive surface uses squircles — softer than a box, more structured than a circle. Difficulty is never a number; it's rendered as a sprout → small tree → full tree, and mood is six simple emoji states (Calm, Nervous, Energetic, Sluggish, Drained, Alert) rather than a slider, which testers found clinical.

Iteration

From a working prototype to a scalable system.

The Challenge screen went through four real passes — not a straight line from "ugly" to "pretty," but from a functional single case to a system that could be applied to dozens of different challenge types.

V1
Content-first pass

A working low-color prototype: bottom nav (Home / Diary / Challenges / Contacts), full instructions and a benefits list. Functional, but text-heavy.

V2
Structured anatomy

Introduced the challenge "anatomy" that shipped: difficulty + duration chips, numbered steps, a dedicated Tips for Success block.

V3
Illustrated warmth

Added the mascot-style character illustrations, circular step icons and an affirmation microcopy card — the point where "calm" started to feel designed, not just absent of noise.

V4
Scaled to the system

The same anatomy applied to a second challenge type ("Compliment a person in your workspace") with photo-based steps and a colorful tag system — proof the pattern held up beyond one hero screen.

Solution

A five-step journey, not a feed.

Every screen exists to move someone from an internal mood check to an external moment of courage, and back to private reflection.

01. Mood Check-In

olo opens with six plain-language states — Calm, Nervous, Energetic, Sluggish, Drained, Alert — instead of a 1–10 slider, which testers read as clinical. The state you pick shapes everything that follows.

Onboarding also asks what brings you here (build confidence, prepare for conversations, make 1–2 friends...) and what feels hardest, so day-one challenges are personalized, not generic.

02. A Matched Challenge

Home surfaces quests "based on your patterns" — e.g. noticing you tend to do better in the evenings, or that cafés feel safer than open spaces for you — and groups challenges by real-world location, like "Local Coffee Shop — 2 quests."

Every challenge carries a difficulty tier (easy/med/deep, shown as sprout → tree) and a duration chip, so the ask is always sized to what you picked at check-in.

03. The Real-World Action

The first quest for every new user is literally "Befriending the Mirror" — practicing a greeting alone before it's aimed at a stranger. From there, challenges scale from "say hi" to "start small talk" to "compliment a coworker."

Tips for Success are concrete, not motivational: "start with people you see regularly," "a smile goes a long way," "you don't need a full conversation — a nod counts."

04. Private Reflection

After a challenge, users record a short voice reflection or tag how it went — private, and never posted anywhere. A real example from testing: "It was nerve-wracking at first, but the barista was really friendly. I felt proud afterwards."

05. Visualized Growth

Progress renders as a puzzle filling in, one piece per completed challenge — a metaphor for "every challenge is a piece, together they tell your story" — instead of a leaderboard or streak counter.

Profile ("Your Space") turns reflections into self-knowledge: best times of day, safest environments, and a notification-tone preference (Gentle Only / Moderate / Brave) the user sets themselves.

Usability Testing

What we tested, and what it actually changed.

Four participants (matched to our four personas) walked through a 7-task protocol against three named criteria. This is the round that killed the timer.

User tasks

  1. Complete onboarding
    Goal: understand personality, comfort zones, social energy
  2. Log your mood
    Goal: test emotional capture and simplicity
  3. Explore your home screen
    Goal: see where users naturally click
  4. Attempt a daily social challenge
    Goal: ensure clarity + emotional comfort
  5. Navigate to the feed and react to a post
    Goal: evaluate fear of judgment + clarity
  6. Use the QR connect flow
    Goal: test social comfort + usability
  7. Check progress and settings in profile
    Goal: validate information hierarchy

Evaluation criteria

Usability Factors

Can users complete tasks without hesitation? Is navigation intuitive? Are labels clear?

Clarity of Purpose

Does the user understand what the app does — and does it feel like it's actually helping introverts grow socially?

Emotional Comfort

Does the app create pressure or relief? Do challenges feel doable, or intimidating?

What all four participants actually told us

The timer had to go
3 of 4 felt uncomfortable or pressured by a countdown — even a short one read as a deadline. Timers were removed from every challenge screen.
Navigation was the strongest part
All 4 independently described it as clean, simple, and easy — validating the flat, four-tab structure and we left it alone.
One difficulty level isn't enough
Participants spanned "extremely shy" to "extrovert" — challenges needed different intensities and starting points, which became the easy/med/deep tiers.
Safe Zone & Cultural Tips over-delivered
All four profiles saw value here and wanted it included — features we'd treated as secondary got promoted to the home screen.
Gamification needs to match personality
Extroverted testers wanted visible small wins; shy testers wanted reassurance, not dopamine hits — informing the private, non-competitive progress model.
The feed wasn't missed
Described as "not of importance right now" — reinforcing the earlier decision to cut a public feed rather than soften it.

Impact & Reflection

Being honest about what this is.

olo was a 2-month independent concept project, not a shipped product — so there's no retention or revenue data to show. What we do have is a concrete before/after, driven by real testing with a small sample.

Before testing

  • Single fixed-intensity daily challenge
  • Countdown timer on the challenge screen
  • Generic motivational tips
  • Public feed as a core surface

After testing

  • Three difficulty tiers (easy / med / deep)
  • No timers anywhere in the product
  • Concrete, situational tips ("start with people you see regularly")
  • Feed removed; Safe Zone + Cultural Tips promoted instead

The directional signal we do have

Across 4 usability sessions and an 18-response concept survey, understanding of the core idea scored 4.00/5 (pre-rename), navigation was independently praised by every tester, and the specific feature testers asked to keep — Safe Zone & Cultural Tips — is now a home-screen surface rather than a buried setting.

Known limitations

  • Small sample: 4 usability participants, 18 survey respondents — directional, not statistically powered.
  • No live build, so no real engagement, retention, or conversion data exists yet.
  • No formal WCAG accessibility audit was run — color-blind and screen-reader testing are still outstanding.
  • The physical puzzle-card concept (below) is unvalidated — it was designed but never user-tested.
Next Steps

Where this goes from here.

  1. Validate accessibility
    Run a full WCAG 2.1 AA pass — contrast, color-blind simulation, and screen-reader labeling — before treating any screen as final.
  2. Widen the sample
    Re-run usability testing with a larger, more demographically varied group beyond the four personas used here.
  3. Pilot quiet connection
    Testers wanted more connection despite rejecting the public feed — worth piloting an opt-in, low-visibility way to meet others rather than assuming the answer is "no social features at all."
  4. Physical puzzle cards
    Unvalidated idea, not a roadmap commitment: each completed puzzle piece could unlock a purchasable physical card — a tangible keepsake bridging digital progress and real-world memory. Would need manufacturing cost research and a willingness-to-pay study before going further.
  5. Instrument a real build
    Replace the target metrics in the Testing section with actual usage data the moment a build exists to measure.
