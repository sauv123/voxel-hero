The Problem

Krizia, a once-revolutionary Italian fashion house has faded into cultural silence.

Founded in 1954 by Mariuccia Mandelli, Krizia was built on intellectual provocation: experimental knits, animal prints, and architectural silhouettes that treated clothing as structure rather than decoration. Yet today the name survives mostly in vintage archives, not contemporary fashion conversation.

The core challenge: how do you resurrect a dormant house without flattening its personality into another tasteful, heritage-brand cliché? Quiet luxury would be the safe, predictable move — but it would erase exactly what made Krizia worth remembering.

Context

Krizia was founded in Milan in 1954 by Mariuccia Mandelli known for experimental knitwear, animal prints, and structural, architecturally minded silhouettes.

The house was genuinely avant-garde in its time: intellectual, a little confrontational, unafraid to treat clothing as an engineering problem rather than a decoration exercise. In recent years the name has largely faded from cultural relevance — the kind of heritage brand that shows up in vintage archives more often than on a runway. That's the real, verifiable starting point for this project: a strong original point of view with almost no contemporary presence to build from.

The Opportunity

Most "quiet luxury" rebrands solve the wrong problem — they trade personality for polish.

The easy version of this project is a minimal wordmark, a beige moodboard, and a promise of "timelessness" — the same move every fashion spec rebrand makes. That would erase exactly what made Krizia interesting: Mandelli's willingness to be a little strange, a little confrontational, in service of an idea about structure.

The brief I set for myself: build a system disciplined enough to read as serious luxury, without sanding off the intellectual edge that gave the original house its identity. Structure, not spectacle — restraint used as a point of view, not a lack of one.

To keep the direction grounded, I framed two loose audience archetypes while designing — a "curator" type who buys 2–3 pieces a year and researches heavily before purchasing, and a design-literate observer who notices construction details other shoppers wouldn't. These are working assumptions used to check tone and copy, not findings from real interviews
Exploration

Three creative directions, developed in parallel before converging.

Rather than picking a single mood board and running with it, I developed three distinct creative territories far enough to test against each other — each with its own visual logic, campaign voice, and rationale.

Chosen direction

The Architect's Clarity

Krizia repositioned around structural honesty — garments photographed and discussed the way a building is: in terms of load, line, and material logic. Concrete, glass, and raw architectural space as the visual world; copy that reads like a design brief, not ad copy. Chosen because it gave the widest room to be both intellectual and visually striking, and because it let the brand's real strength — structural silhouette — become the entire story instead of a footnote.

Explored, not chosen

The Irregular Mirror

A stranger, more surreal territory — distorted reflections, warped proportion, the idea that "truth lives in the detail" precisely because the overall silhouette lies to you first. More provocative than Architect's Clarity, but harder to sustain across practical applications like packaging and tags without losing legibility.

Explored, not chosen

Logic, Warped

Leaned into absurdism and Gen-Z-coded irony as a reaction against algorithmic sameness in luxury marketing — the most conceptually daring option, and the one most likely to read as costume rather than clothing if executed at full intensity. Elements of its wit survived into the final tone of voice in smaller doses.

The Structural Index™

Transparency as luxury. An NFC-enabled garment passport revealing pleating density, material composition, and origin verification.

The Drafting Table

Retail as an architect's studio. A hybrid environment between material laboratory and gallery space for technical studies.

The Alchemic Material Library

Material as experience. An interactive installation showcasing cork polymer composites, metallic fabrics, and bio-engineered materials.

Identity System

A wordmark built like a structural drawing, not a signature.

01

Wordmark & Tagline

The custom geometric letterforms deliberately avoid the swash and softness common to fashion wordmarks — sharp terminals, consistent stroke logic, built more like technical lettering than calligraphy. Paired with a single line that carries the whole strategy: "What remains is structure."

Color & Type Tokens

A near-black base with a single petrol-blue accent, used deliberately sparingly — the opposite of the multi-accent palettes common in fashion branding. Space Grotesk carries both display and body roles, keeping the system to one typeface family rather than a display/body pairing.

Secondary Mark & Application Details

A radiating starburst emblem functions as a secondary mark for garment tags and hardware — a nod to the brand's founding year rather than a literal logo replacement. Care labels carry a fully custom pictogram set instead of licensed washing-symbol icons, so even a utilitarian tag reads as part of the same design language.

Result

Testing the system across the touchpoints that actually make or break a brand.

A logo is easy to make look good in isolation. The real test is whether the same voice survives contact with an invitation, a billboard, a tote bag, and a phone screen without feeling like four different studios worked on it.

01

Campaign & Event

An event invitation framed as an access pass rather than a party invite — "Krizia invites you to a study in form," stamped "Access Granted" — and a billboard campaign built from the same fragmented, architectural-copy voice ("Form speaks softly," "Truth lives in the detail").

Editorial

Product & Retail

An oversized, cropped "K" on a tote bag borrows scale from the billboard work rather than shrinking the logo down politely — the same confidence applied to a cheap, high-frequency product as to the hero campaign.

Reflection

What I learned and what comes next

What Worked

  • Starting with research12 interviews revealed the core problem quickly
  • Decision matrix for strategyMade positioning choice objective, not subjective
  • Testing early and often8 participants caught critical issues before launch
02

What I'd Do Differently

  • Involve engineers earlierMaterial library development took longer than estimated
